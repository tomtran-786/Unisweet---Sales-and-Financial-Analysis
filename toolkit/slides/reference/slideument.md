# The slideument problem

The parts of deck-building that are decisions, not code. Distilled from
`from-blah-to-boardroom.md` (Hern Lee, *From Blah to Boardroom*) and
`storytelling-with-data-p3.md` (Knaflic, *Storytelling with Data*, Wiley 2015,
Part 3 — case studies).

## 1. The continuum

Communication mechanism runs on a line, not a switch:

```
live presentation ─────────────────────────────► written document / email
you control pace                                  reader controls pace
slides stay sparse                                detail must be on the page
you answer the questions                          the page answers them
```

The two ends want genuinely different artifacts. In an ideal world you would
build both. Nobody has the time, so people build one thing aimed at the middle —
the **slideument**: part presentation, part document, serving neither.

Knaflic names the circulated audience precisely: a pre-read, a takeaway, *or
those who were not able to attend*. Same content used for both ends is typically
too detailed to project and not detailed enough to stand alone.

## 2. The resolution

Do not pick a point on the continuum. Write the content once and render it twice.

- **Live rendering** — action title and the visual. Everything else is in the
  speaker notes, where it prompts you and does not crowd the screen.
- **Circulated rendering** — the same title and visual, plus the takeaway on the
  page and the narration under it. The annotations do what your voice did.

One spec means the two cannot disagree with each other, which is the failure mode
of maintaining a "presentation version" and a "document version" by hand.

**Bias toward standalone when unsure.** Boardroom's argument is about risk, not
preference: execs interrupt, show up late, change the agenda, or cancel and ask
for the file. If any of that is plausible, build for the reader. The cost is a
slight bias toward self-enclosed sentences rather than fragment bullets — not a
wall of text.

## 3. Annotation versus animation

The two sources conflict, and it is a useful conflict.

**Knaflic:** reveal the visual in steps live, colour directing attention to one
component at a time; then compress every step into one comprehensive annotated
visual for circulation.

**Boardroom:** do not animate at all. In front of executives you do not control
the flow — they interject, and answering from the middle of a multi-click build
means hunting for the right slide. Its stated exception is a setting where you
present unilaterally and nobody interrupts, such as a townhall. Its preferred
tool is callout boxes on a single slide, explicitly because keeping the linkage
between messages visible beats splitting them across pseudo-animated duplicates.

**Resolution.** Build the compressed annotated visual and keep it on one slide.
It is simultaneously Knaflic's circulated artifact and Boardroom's foolproof live
slide. Treat pseudo-animation as a special case for talks you fully control.

The practical consequence: put the work into the *annotation layer* — callout
boxes, direct labels, a stated takeaway — rather than into build sequences. The
annotation layer is the thing that survives being forwarded.

## 4. Structure

**Pyramid Principle, always.** Lead with the answer, then the supporting
arguments, then the underlying data. Minto: you think from the bottom up, but you
present from the top down. The instinct to walk through data → insight →
conclusion is a research log; invert it.

**Order by importance, urgency or impact** — an explicit choice each time, not
the sequence the analysis happened to run in.

**Repetition is not redundancy.** Executive summary → detail → recap. It feels
repetitive to the author and is what makes the message stick for everyone else.

**Signposts.** In a multi-section deck, show where the reader is. Uncertainty
about whether they are near the start or the end is a live distraction.

**Appendix and pre-read are structural tools, not dumping grounds.** Supporting
detail that establishes credibility goes in the appendix and gets referenced, not
walked through. Context the audience must have in advance goes in a pre-read so
live time is spent on the hard-hitting parts.

**Cut slides until you cannot.** And on what remains: every slide should be
selling something — an ask, a decision, a reframe. If a slide sells nothing, cut
it too.

## 5. Headlines

**Write them first.** They take seconds to draft and hours to get right, and they
are the part of the deck most likely to be the only part read.

Two interrogation questions for any deck:

1. Delete everything but the headlines. Do they still tell a coherent, tight
   story? (This is *horizontal logic*.)
2. Does the content on each slide actually support that slide's headline?
   (*Vertical logic*.)

Three quality tests for each headline:

| Test | Fails as | Passes as |
|---|---|---|
| **Strong** | passive, or a claim nobody would dispute | action-led, assertive, contestable |
| **Direct** | corporate register that must be decoded | plain words |
| **Crisp** | short but flat — "DAUs have trended down three quarters" | short and pointed — "we're losing users" |

**Commentate, don't just label.** Labelling states what a thing is; commentating
states what it means. Charts still need proper labels — but headers and captions
are the chance to say something, and spending them on a description wastes the
most valuable line on the page.

**Tone is a deliberate choice.** The same slide reads as confident or as
cautiously-optimistic-with-dependencies depending only on the headline. Neither is
wrong. Pick the one that matches the message you intend, and pick it on purpose.

## 6. Final checks

- **Titles only, top to bottom.** They should be the argument, unaided.
- **Cold read.** Hand the circulated rendering to someone with no context and ask
  what they conclude and what they would ask. If they cannot state the ask, the
  deck does not have one yet.
- **The forward test.** Imagine the meeting is cancelled an hour before and you
  have to send the file instead. Does it still work? If not, fix that before
  polishing anything.
